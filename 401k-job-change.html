What Happens to Your 401(k) When You Change Jobs

Sept 12, 2026, 6 min read

Your 401(k) balance is yours regardless of who you work for now — leaving a job doesn't forfeit the money. What it does trigger is a decision, usually within a specific window, about where that money lives next. Doing nothing is technically an option too, but it's rarely the best one.

Your four real options

  • Leave it with your old employer's plan: Often allowed if your balance is above a small threshold (commonly $5,000+). Simple, but you'll be tracking multiple accounts over a career, and some old plans have higher fees than what's currently available to you.
  • Roll it into your new employer's 401(k): Consolidates your retirement savings into one account, if your new plan accepts rollovers and its investment options and fees are reasonable.
  • Roll it into an IRA: Usually gives you far more investment choices than any single employer plan, and keeps the tax-deferred (or Roth) status intact if done as a direct rollover.
  • Cash it out: Almost always the wrong move before retirement age — you'll typically owe income tax on the full amount plus a 10% early withdrawal penalty, on top of losing decades of potential growth.

The rollover mechanics that actually matter

A direct rollover — where the money moves institution-to-institution without ever passing through your hands — avoids withholding taxes and penalties entirely. An indirect rollover, where a check is sent to you first, usually has 20% withheld automatically, and you have 60 days to deposit the full original amount (including the withheld portion, from your own pocket) into a new account or owe taxes and penalties on the shortfall. Always ask for a direct rollover.

Leaving a job is a natural moment to check whether you're still getting the full employer match at your new plan — a surprising number of people forget to update this after switching jobs.

Small old accounts are easy to lose track of

A $2,000 balance in a plan from a job you left five years ago is easy to forget entirely, especially if you've moved or changed your email since. Consolidating small old 401(k)s into your current plan or an IRA isn't just tidier — it makes the money far less likely to become genuinely lost, unclaimed retirement savings.

What to actually do this week

If you've changed jobs in the last year or two, log into any old 401(k) accounts to confirm you still have access, then decide between rolling into your new plan or an IRA based on which has better investment options and lower fees. Either beats leaving it on autopilot indefinitely.
