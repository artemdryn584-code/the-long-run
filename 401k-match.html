The 401(k) Match Most People Leave on the Table

Sept 2, 2026, 5 min read, Updated Sept 2026

A 401(k) match is money your employer adds to your retirement account, on top of your salary, when you contribute your own money first. If you're not contributing enough to get the full match, you're turning down part of your compensation — not saving money, just not collecting it.

How a typical match works

A common structure is "50% of the first 6% of your salary" — meaning if you put in 6% of your paycheck, your employer adds another 3%. Structures vary a lot, so the number that matters is written in your plan documents, not in general advice like this.

Where to find your actual match

  • Log into your 401(k) provider's website (Fidelity, Vanguard, Empower, and similar all show this on the plan summary page).
  • Look for "employer match" or "company match" in the plan details, not just your account balance.
  • Ask HR directly if it isn't clear — this is a completely normal question and they answer it often.

Why this beats almost every other financial move

A 50% match is an immediate 50% return on your money before it's even invested. No stock, bond, or savings account reliably matches that. For most households, getting the full employer match should happen before extra debt payoff or other investing — the one common exception is high-interest credit card debt, which usually costs more than the match is worth.

Missing your full match isn't a savings choice — it's unclaimed pay.

What to do if you can't afford the full match right now

Contribute whatever percentage you can, even if it's below the match threshold, and set a reminder to increase it by 1% every few months or with each raise. Many plans have an auto-escalation feature that does this automatically — worth turning on if it exists.

A five-minute check

Open your most recent pay stub, find your 401(k) contribution percentage, and compare it to your plan's match formula. If you're below the threshold, increasing your contribution by even 1-2% today is one of the highest-value five-minute tasks in personal finance.
