Cost of Living by State: What Actually Drives the Difference

Sept 12, 2026, 6 min read, Updated Sept 2026

Key takeaways

  • Housing is usually the biggest driver of cost-of-living differences between states.
  • Childcare, healthcare and insurance, transportation, and state/local taxes also shift meaningfully by location.
  • Comparing raw salaries across locations is misleading — a cost-of-living-adjusted comparison is far more useful.
  • Check local housing costs, tax rates, childcare, and commute costs for the specific area, not just the state average.

Two job offers, same salary, different states — and yet one of them is obviously the better deal once you actually sit down and think about it. "It depends where you live" isn't a dodge; it's the correct answer, and it comes down to a short, checkable list rather than a vague sense that one place is "more expensive."

What to actually check before comparing locations

  1. Typical housing costs (rent or purchase price) for the specific neighborhood, not just the state average.
  2. Local income, sales, and property tax rates.
  3. Childcare costs, if relevant to your household.
  4. Typical commute and transportation costs for where you'd actually live relative to work.
  5. A cost-of-living-adjusted salary comparison tool, rather than comparing raw offers directly.

Why the list is shaped that way

Housing drives most of the gap — a comparable home or apartment can cost multiples more in a high-demand metro than in a lower-cost region, and since it's typically the largest single expense in any budget, that one factor alone explains much of the difference between "comfortable" and "tight" at similar incomes. Behind it: childcare (which can rival a mortgage payment in some high-cost areas), healthcare and insurance premiums, transportation in car-dependent areas versus cities with transit, and the local mix of income, sales, and property tax — see our guide on what no state income tax actually means for one piece of that last one.

Which is also why comparing two raw salary numbers is misleading in the first place: a higher salary in a high-cost city can leave less real disposable income than a lower one in a cheaper region once these costs are factored in. A cost-of-living-adjusted comparison tool, checked against current local data for the specific area, beats comparing offers directly.

Two people earning the same salary in different states aren't actually earning the same amount, once what that money has to cover is factored in.

Frequently asked questions

What's the single biggest driver of cost-of-living differences between states?

Housing, usually by a wide margin — a comparable home or apartment can cost multiples more in a high-demand metro area than in a lower-cost region, and since housing is typically the largest expense in most budgets, it explains much of the difference between comfortable and tight at similar income levels.

What other costs vary a lot by location?

Childcare, which can rival a mortgage payment in some high-cost areas; healthcare and insurance premiums; transportation, especially in car-dependent areas with long commutes; and state and local income, sales, and property taxes.

Why shouldn't I just compare salary offers directly between cities?

A higher salary in a high-cost city can leave less real disposable income than a lower salary in a cheaper region once housing and other locally-variable costs are factored in — a cost-of-living-adjusted comparison tool is far more useful than comparing raw numbers.

What should I check before comparing two locations?

Typical housing costs for the specific neighborhood (not just the state average), local income, sales, and property tax rates, childcare costs if relevant, typical commute and transportation costs, and a cost-of-living-adjusted salary comparison.

How much does living in a state with no income tax help?

It's one factor among several — see our guide on what no state income tax actually means, since housing and other costs often matter more than income tax savings alone.
