Open Enrollment: How to Actually Choose Your Health Insurance Plan

Sept 12, 2026, 7 min read

Open enrollment — typically in November for many employer plans — is the one window each year to change health insurance without a qualifying life event. Because it's often rushed at the end of a busy month, many people simply re-select last year's plan without checking whether it's still the best fit. A slightly slower look can save real money over the following year.

Premium isn't the only cost that matters

The monthly premium is the most visible number, but total annual cost also depends on the deductible (what you pay before insurance kicks in), copays, coinsurance, and the out-of-pocket maximum (the most you'd pay in a worst-case year). A lower premium with a much higher deductible can cost more overall if you expect meaningful medical expenses.

Match the plan to how you actually use healthcare

  • Rarely visit the doctor, healthy year expected: A high-deductible plan with a lower premium, often paired with an HSA, can make sense.
  • Ongoing prescriptions, planned procedures, or a growing family: A plan with a higher premium but lower deductible and copays often costs less in total once predictable costs are factored in.
  • Specific doctors or specialists you want to keep: Confirm they're in-network for any plan you're considering — switching plans to save on premium is a bad trade if it means losing an established provider relationship.

HSA vs. FSA, if offered alongside your plan

A Health Savings Account (HSA), available with qualifying high-deductible plans, lets contributions roll over year to year and can even be invested for the long term. A Flexible Spending Account (FSA) usually has a "use it or lose it" rule each year. If your plan qualifies for an HSA, it's often worth prioritizing for its combination of tax advantages and flexibility.

The plan you had last year isn't wrong to keep — but it's worth being a decision you made this year, not a default you didn't revisit.

Questions worth answering before re-enrolling

  1. Did your healthcare needs change this year — new prescriptions, a planned procedure, a growing family?
  2. Are your current doctors still in-network for the plans available to you?
  3. Has your income or tax situation changed in a way that affects whether an HSA-eligible plan makes more sense?
  4. What's the total realistic annual cost (premium plus expected out-of-pocket) for each option, not just the premium?

A reasonable amount of time to spend

Thirty minutes comparing 2-3 realistic plan options against last year's actual healthcare usage is usually enough to catch the cases where switching (or staying) clearly makes sense — far less time than the cost of an ill-fitting plan over the following twelve months.
