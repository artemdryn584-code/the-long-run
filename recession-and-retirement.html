What a Recession Actually Means for Your Retirement Account

Sept 12, 2026, 6 min read

Watching a retirement account balance drop 20-30% during a recession is unsettling no matter how many times you've read that markets recover. What that drop actually means for you, though, depends heavily on one thing: how many years remain before you need to start withdrawing the money.

If retirement is 10+ years away

A market downturn happening now is, counterintuitively, not bad news for your long-term outcome — it's an opportunity. Regular contributions during a downturn buy shares at lower prices, and historically, markets have recovered and gone on to new highs well before a decade passes. The balance number looks worse today; the eventual outcome usually isn't.

If retirement is 3-10 years away

This is the range where a recession deserves real attention, not panic. It's worth checking that your portfolio's stock allocation actually matches your timeline — see our guide on how much risk to take by age and timeline — but a downturn this many years out still leaves meaningful time to recover before you need the money.

If you're retired or retiring very soon

This is where a recession has the most real impact, through something called "sequence of returns risk" — withdrawing money from a shrunken portfolio locks in losses in a way that doesn't apply if you're still years from touching the money. People in or entering this stage often benefit from having 1-3 years of planned withdrawals in cash or stable holdings specifically so they're not forced to sell stocks at a loss during a downturn.

A recession doesn't ask how you feel about your balance. It asks whether you actually need to sell anything right now — and for most people who aren't retired yet, the honest answer is no.

The single worst move during a downturn

Selling out of the market after a large drop — locking in the loss — and then staying in cash until things "feel safe again" is the move that turns a temporary paper loss into a permanent one. Markets tend to recover before sentiment does, which means waiting to feel confident again usually means missing much of the recovery.

What's actually worth doing

  • If you're years from retirement: keep contributing on schedule, and avoid checking the balance daily.
  • If you're within a decade: confirm your allocation still matches your actual timeline.
  • If you're retired or close to it: make sure near-term spending needs aren't sitting entirely in stocks.
