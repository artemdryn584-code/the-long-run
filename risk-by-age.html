How Much Risk Should You Actually Be Taking, By Age

Sept 12, 2026, 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Age-based rules like "110 minus your age" are a rough starting point, not a precise formula.
  • What actually matters is your real time horizon for each pool of money, not your birth year.
  • Money needed within 1-3 years belongs in cash regardless of age; money needed 10+ years out can absorb more stock volatility, also regardless of age.
  • Rebalancing once or twice a year keeps your actual risk level from drifting away from your target mix.

"Subtract your age from 110 (or 120) to get your stock allocation percentage" is one of the most repeated rules in personal finance. It's a reasonable starting point, but treating it as a precise formula misses the actual variable that matters: how many years until you'll need the money.

Why the rule exists at all

Stocks are volatile in the short term but have historically grown faster than bonds or cash over long periods. The rule assumes younger investors have decades to ride out downturns, while people closer to retirement have less time to recover from a bad few years — so the mix shifts gradually from more stocks toward more bonds as retirement nears.

Where "by age" breaks down

  • Two 45-year-olds with different retirement ages (55 vs. 70) have very different real timelines, even though the rule treats them identically.
  • Money in a taxable brokerage account you plan to use for a house down payment in 3 years shouldn't be invested the same way as retirement money 30 years out — even if it's the same person's age.
  • Risk tolerance (how you actually react to a 30% drop) and risk capacity (how much loss your timeline can financially absorb) are different things, and age-based rules only really speak to the second one.

A more useful question than "how old am I"

For each pool of money, ask: when will I actually need to spend this? Money needed within 1-3 years generally belongs in cash or very stable holdings, regardless of age. Money needed in 10+ years can generally absorb more stock market volatility, again regardless of age. Retirement accounts you won't touch for decades can typically hold more risk than a rule based purely on your current age would suggest.

Age is a rough proxy for time horizon. When you know your actual time horizon, use that instead — it's the thing the rule was trying to estimate in the first place.

What this looks like in practice

A 35-year-old with a stable job and a 30-year runway to retirement might reasonably hold 80-90% stocks in their retirement accounts. A 60-year-old planning to retire at 62 might hold closer to 50-60% stocks, shifting further toward bonds and cash as the date to start withdrawing gets closer. Neither number is "correct" for everyone that age — they depend on the actual plan, not the birthday.

One thing worth doing regardless of your target mix

Whatever allocation you land on, rebalancing once or twice a year — selling a bit of whatever's grown to be overweight and buying more of what's underweight — keeps your actual risk level from silently drifting away from your intended one as markets move.

Frequently asked questions

What does the "110 minus your age" rule mean?

It's a rule of thumb suggesting you subtract your age from 110 (or 120) to get the percentage of your portfolio that should be in stocks, with the rest in bonds or cash.

Is an age-based rule accurate for everyone the same age?

No. Two people the same age can have very different actual timelines — different planned retirement ages or different goals for the money — so the rule only approximates what really matters, which is time horizon.

What's the difference between risk tolerance and risk capacity?

Risk tolerance is how you emotionally react to a drop like a 30% decline. Risk capacity is how much loss your actual timeline can financially absorb. Age-based rules mostly speak to risk capacity, not tolerance.

How often should I rebalance my portfolio?

Once or twice a year is generally enough to keep your actual allocation from drifting away from your intended target as markets move.

How much in stocks might a 35-year-old with a 30-year runway hold?

Such an investor might reasonably hold 80-90% stocks in retirement accounts, though the right number depends on the actual plan, not just the age.
