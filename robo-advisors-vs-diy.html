Robo-Advisors vs. DIY Investing: What's the Real Difference

Sept 12, 2026, 6 min read, Updated Sept 2026

Key takeaways

  • Robo-advisors charge roughly 0.25%-0.50% a year to build, rebalance, and often tax-loss harvest a diversified portfolio for you.
  • DIY investing in a few broad index funds costs only the funds' own expense ratios, often 0.03%-0.10%, with no advisory fee.
  • A robo-advisor's fee is often worth it if the alternative is not investing at all, or panic-selling during downturns.
  • The real comparison isn't robo-advisor versus DIY in the abstract — it's robo-advisor versus what you would actually do without one.

A robo-advisor builds and manages a diversified portfolio for you automatically, based on a short questionnaire about your goals and risk tolerance, for a small annual fee (commonly 0.25%-0.50% of your balance). DIY investing means picking and managing your own mix of index funds yourself. For many people, the two approaches end up holding nearly identical underlying investments — the difference is really about who does the work.

What a robo-advisor actually does for its fee

  • Builds a diversified portfolio of low-cost funds matched to your risk tolerance and timeline.
  • Automatically rebalances the portfolio as markets move, without you having to remember to do it.
  • Often includes tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts — automatically selling losing positions to offset gains, which can be genuinely difficult to do well manually.

What DIY investing saves you

Managing your own portfolio of two or three broad index funds costs only the funds' own expense ratios — often 0.03%-0.10% — with no additional advisory fee. Over decades, the gap between a 0.05% DIY portfolio and a 0.35% robo-advisor fee compounds into a real difference, though it requires you to actually do the rebalancing and stay disciplined during downturns.

Where a robo-advisor tends to be worth the fee

If the alternative to a robo-advisor isn't "confident DIY investing" but "not investing at all because it feels overwhelming," or if you know you'd panic-sell during a downturn without some structure in place, the fee is buying real behavioral value — a lower-cost fund sitting in cash because you're too intimidated to buy it earns nothing.

The real comparison isn't robo-advisor versus DIY in the abstract. It's robo-advisor versus what you would actually do without one.

Where DIY tends to make more sense

  • You're comfortable picking 2-4 broad index funds and rebalancing once or twice a year — a genuinely simple, learnable task.
  • Your account balance is large enough that the percentage fee adds up to a meaningful dollar amount each year.
  • You want full control over which specific funds you hold, for tax or personal preference reasons.

A reasonable way to decide

If reading the section above on DIY index investing felt approachable and mildly boring, you'll probably do fine managing it yourself. If it felt like a chore you'd keep postponing, a robo-advisor's fee is a reasonable price for automatically getting invested and staying that way — which matters far more than optimizing the fee down to the decimal.

Frequently asked questions

How much do robo-advisors typically charge?

Commonly 0.25%-0.50% of your account balance per year, on top of the underlying funds' own expense ratios.

How much does DIY index investing cost?

Just the expense ratios of the funds you hold, often 0.03%-0.10%, with no additional advisory fee.

What is tax-loss harvesting?

A strategy, often automated by robo-advisors, of selling losing positions in a taxable account to offset gains — something that's genuinely difficult to do well manually.

When is a robo-advisor worth the fee?

When the alternative isn't confident DIY investing but not investing at all because it feels overwhelming, or when you'd likely panic-sell during a downturn without some structure in place.

How do I decide between a robo-advisor and DIY investing?

If picking and rebalancing 2-4 broad index funds sounds approachable and mildly boring, DIY will probably work fine. If it sounds like a chore you'd keep postponing, the robo-advisor fee is a reasonable price for staying invested.
