Student Loan Repayment in 2026: A Plain-English Overview of Your Options

Sept 12, 2026, 7 min read

Student loan repayment rules have shifted enough times over the past few years that even people who've been paying diligently often aren't sure which plan they're on, or whether it's still the right one. This isn't a complete legal breakdown — for that, your loan servicer's account portal has the specifics for your exact loans. It's a map of the main paths, so you know what questions to ask.

Federal loans: know your servicer and your plan type

The first step is confirming who services your loan (this can change — servicers have shifted several times in recent years) and which repayment plan you're currently enrolled in. Log into studentaid.gov to see both, since a loan that transferred servicers sometimes reverts to a default plan without much notice.

The standard plan vs. income-driven plans

  • Standard repayment: Fixed payments over 10 years. The most predictable option, and usually the cheapest overall if you can afford the monthly amount.
  • Income-driven repayment (IDR): Payments are set as a percentage of your discretionary income, and the loan is forgiven after 20-25 years of qualifying payments. This lowers monthly payments but can mean paying more interest over time.

Income-driven plans make the most sense when your income is genuinely low relative to your loan balance — for example, early in a career or in a lower-paying field despite a large loan balance. They make less sense if you can comfortably afford the standard payment, since you'd likely pay more in total interest for a lower monthly number you didn't need.

Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)

If you work full-time for a qualifying government or nonprofit employer, PSLF forgives remaining federal loan balances after 120 qualifying monthly payments. The most common mistake isn't with the employer or the payments — it's not submitting the employer certification form annually, which is how the loan servicer confirms your progress is actually being counted.

The most expensive student loan mistake isn't a high interest rate. It's not knowing which plan you're on until you've missed years of a program you would have qualified for.

Refinancing with a private lender

Refinancing can lower your interest rate if your credit and income have improved since you took out the loans, but it converts federal loans into a private loan — permanently giving up income-driven plans, PSLF eligibility, and federal deferment options. This makes the most sense for loans you're confident you'll pay off well before you'd ever need those protections — similar in spirit to consolidating other high-interest debt, where you're trading flexibility for a potentially better rate.

A realistic starting checklist

  1. Log into studentaid.gov and confirm your loan types, balances, and current repayment plan.
  2. Check whether your employer qualifies for PSLF before considering refinancing anything.
  3. Compare your current monthly payment to what you can actually afford — not what you're used to.
  4. Only refinance federal loans if you're certain you won't need income-driven or forgiveness options later.

None of these paths is universally "best" — the standard plan, an income-driven plan, PSLF, and refinancing all fit different situations well. The costly outcome is picking one by default and never revisiting it as your income or job changes.
