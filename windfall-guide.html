What to Do With a Windfall (Bonus, Inheritance, Tax Refund) Before You Spend It

Sept 12, 2026, 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Wait 2-4 weeks before deciding what to do with a windfall to remove the impulse-spending window entirely.
  • A reasonable order of operations: set aside taxes, top up your emergency fund, pay off high-interest debt, invest for long-term goals, then spend a defined portion on purpose.
  • Deciding in advance on a guilt-free "fun" portion, like 10%, actually makes it easier to stick to a responsible plan for the rest.
  • Inheritances deserve extra patience — months, not just weeks — since grief-driven decisions are more likely to be regretted later.

A work bonus, a tax refund, an inheritance, or a cash gift all share one dangerous quality: they weren't part of your regular budget, so they don't feel like "real" money the way a paycheck does. That's exactly why windfalls are so easy to spend without much to show for it afterward — and exactly why a brief pause before spending pays off.

Wait before deciding anything

Parking the money in a savings account for even two to four weeks before making any decisions removes the impulse-spending window entirely, without costing you anything. Large windfalls in particular — an inheritance, a big bonus — deserve this pause more, not less, since the decisions involved (paying off debt, investing, big purchases) are harder to undo.

A reasonable order of operations

  1. Set aside taxes if relevant: Some windfalls (bonuses, certain inheritances) can carry tax implications — check before assuming the full amount is spendable.
  2. Top up your emergency fund if it's thin: A windfall is one of the fastest ways to build a real cushion without months of gradual saving.
  3. Pay off high-interest debt: Credit card balances above roughly 8-10% interest are a close to guaranteed return when paid off.
  4. Invest for long-term goals: Once the above are handled, retirement accounts or a taxable brokerage account put the money to work for years to come.
  5. Spend some of it on purpose: A defined, guilt-free portion spent deliberately tends to prevent the whole amount from quietly leaking away later.

The problem with most windfalls isn't a single bad purchase. It's a dozen small ones that add up to the money simply being gone, with no clear decision behind any of it.

The "some of it on purpose" step matters more than it sounds

Deciding in advance that, say, 10% of a windfall is earmarked for something enjoyable — with no guilt attached — makes the more responsible use of the other 90% far easier to stick to. Treating the entire amount as off-limits fun money often backfires into spending more than that 10% anyway, just without a plan.

Inheritances deserve extra patience

Money inherited after a loss carries emotional weight that a bonus or tax refund doesn't, and decisions made quickly during grief are more likely to be regretted later. Beyond the general order of operations above, it's reasonable to let a larger inheritance sit for a few months — not just weeks — before making any major, hard-to-reverse decisions with it.

The one-sentence rule

Before spending a windfall, write down in one sentence what you're doing with it and why — "$2,000 to the emergency fund because it's currently under one month of expenses" is a plan; "I'll figure it out" is how windfalls disappear.
